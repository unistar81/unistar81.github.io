Confirmation

Confirmation introduces a deliberate decision point before an action whose consequence, scope, or cost deserves explicit review. It should reduce consequential mistakes without turning routine work into repeated friction.

Composition boundary

Confirmation owns the decision before commitment, not the dialog mechanics or the underlying action. Button owns each action control. Action Groups owns hierarchy between confirm and cancel paths. Modal owns dialog semantics, focus containment, dismissal mechanics, and focus return when the decision must block the rest of the interface. The consuming application owns the actual operation and its success or failure.

Status Messaging or other feedback patterns communicate the result after commitment. Do not use Confirmation merely to announce something that has already happened.

When confirmation is warranted

  • Confirm meaningful consequences, not routine clicks. Use confirmation when an action is difficult to reverse, affects a broad scope, creates an external side effect, changes access or publication state, or otherwise carries a consequence users reasonably need to review.
  • Prefer prevention over repeated prompts. Clear labels, constrained choices, undo, autosave, review screens, and reversible workflows may prevent mistakes more effectively than another confirmation step.
  • Do not confirm ordinary reversible actions by default. Repeated low-value prompts train users to dismiss dialogs without reading them and weaken the signal when a consequential confirmation genuinely matters.
  • Confirm the final commitment point. Avoid asking users to confirm the same decision more than once unless the later step introduces new consequence or scope.
  • Do not use confirmation as acknowledgment. If there is no real choice and the user can only dismiss an informational message, use the appropriate feedback or disclosure pattern instead.

Decision content

  • State the action in the title. Name what is about to happen rather than using generic titles such as "Are you sure?"
  • Explain the consequence before the actions. Identify the affected scope, permanence, external effect, or other information necessary to make an informed decision.
  • Keep supporting content proportional to the decision. Confirmation is not a substitute for a long review flow. When users must compare substantial information before commitment, provide that review in the surrounding workflow.
  • Use explicit action labels. Prefer labels such as "Publish changes" and "Keep editing" over "Yes," "No," "OK," or "Cancel" when a more specific result can be named.
  • Keep the cancel path semantically honest. Closing or dismissing the confirmation should normally mean abandoning the confirmation and returning to the prior state, not silently performing another action.

Modal & focus behavior

  • Use Modal only when the decision must block background interaction. Inline or page-level confirmation may be appropriate when the decision belongs naturally in the current flow and does not require modality.
  • Let Modal own dialog mechanics. When modal, use the canonical Modal contract for dialog semantics, focus containment, Escape/close behavior, scroll behavior, and focus return.
  • Choose initial focus deliberately. Do not automatically focus the confirming action. For high-consequence decisions, a safe/cancel action or non-action context target may be the more appropriate initial focus when that reduces accidental commitment.
  • Treat dismissal as the non-commit path. Escape, close, or outside-dismiss behavior must not commit the consequential action. Use only dismissal mechanisms supported by the canonical Modal contract.
  • Return focus predictably. After dismissal or completion, restore focus through the Modal contract to a logical location in the originating workflow.

Commitment & outcome

  • Prevent duplicate commitment when repeated activation would be unsafe. Pending state belongs to the consuming application; preserve the initiating action in a predictable location while work is in progress.
  • Do not imply success before the operation succeeds. If commitment can fail, keep the outcome understandable and provide a recovery path rather than dismissing the decision surface in a way that leaves status ambiguous.
  • Use feedback after commitment. Compose with Loading & Progress for meaningful waiting and Status Messaging for completion, failure, or other resulting state.
  • Preserve the user's context on failure. A failed operation should not force users to reconstruct the decision or lose information they need to retry.
  • Confirmation does not make an unsafe action safe. Authorization, validation, business rules, and other safeguards remain the responsibility of the underlying workflow.
